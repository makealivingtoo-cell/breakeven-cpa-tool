7 website checks before you spend more on leads

An electrician, plumber, painter or renovation contractor doesn't need a giant marketing dashboard to spot common contact-path problems. Start with these seven checks, using only what an ordinary customer can see.

This checklist flags possible friction, not proof of missed customers. Real conversion performance requires your own records and consented analytics.

01. Say what you do and where you do it

Your first screen should identify your main service and service area. “Residential electrician in Toronto, North York and Scarborough” explains more than a generic promise to deliver excellence.

Test: Could a first-time visitor answer “Can you do my type of job in my area?” without scrolling?

02. Give mobile visitors one obvious next step

Try your own site on a phone. Are your call and quote buttons easy to find and tap? Does every service page tell visitors what to do next?

Test: Time how long it takes to get from a service page to a working phone, quote, or contact action.

03. Check that every contact detail actually works

Inspect the displayed email address, the destination of its link, click-to-call buttons, and inquiry forms. A leftover template address or form that never reaches the right inbox can quietly waste genuine interest.

Test: Send yourself one legitimate test inquiry, then confirm it arrived at the intended inbox. Don't fabricate leads or send test messages to other businesses.

04. Set clear response expectations

Tell people what happens after they request a quote: when you'll normally acknowledge it, whether photos help, and what information you need before scheduling. Only promise response times you can actually meet.

Possible copy: “Tell us your address, the type of work and your preferred time. We'll confirm the next step during business hours.”

05. Put trust proof near the point of decision

Place real licenses, relevant insurance information, project examples or verified customer reviews close to the contact action. Keep credentials current and never invent testimonials.

06. Follow up on quotes customers actually requested

Where you have a legitimate customer relationship and the necessary consent, give people a useful reminder rather than a pressure-heavy sales sequence. Keep a clear opt-out channel where required.

Three-message quote follow-up example
  1. After sending a quote: “Your estimate is ready. Let us know if anything needs clarification.”
  2. After an appropriate interval: “Do you have questions about the scope or timing? Happy to clarify.”
  3. Final check: “Would you like us to hold this estimate or close it for now? Either answer is fine.”

Adapt timing and consent practices to your business and applicable law; this is not permission to send unsolicited messages.

07. Measure the path, not a guess about revenue

For each month, count actual inquiries, responded-to inquiries, quotes sent and jobs booked. Separate phone, form and email sources where possible. Use a consistent definition before assigning dollar values to missed opportunities.

Start simple: A spreadsheet with inquiry date, source, first response date, quote date and outcome is enough to identify real patterns.
